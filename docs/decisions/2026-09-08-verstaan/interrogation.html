Can a hand-written UNL engine still earn its place next to a 40 MB offline NMT model?

Yes, on the terms you set: as a deterministic engine that is the product, not a means to one. Build the UNL-native engine, mirror the whole archive rather than three languages, and license it as Shared Files: an MPL-2.0 engine, CC BY-SA data, a contributor agreement, and a trademark on the name. The interrogation is closed. The recommendation section says why, what the runner-up is, and what would change the answer.

01

What I think you're really asking

The question is no longer "rules or neural", and it is no longer "rules with or without a fallback". You have chosen rules first, with neural held in reserve until the rules are measured to fall short. The question is now how to structure a repository whose data half is CC BY-SA and whose code half is yours, so that an open initiative can grow on it without you losing the freedom to change the code licence later, and how to bring the archive home so the project does not depend on a volunteer-run site staying up.

Assumption A, verified. The UNL archive is live at unlarchive.org, publicly crawlable, and its materials are released under CC BY-SA unless an item states otherwise. Login credentials exist and are held by you, not in this document.
Assumption B, still unmeasured. A compiled rule engine is smaller and faster than a quantised neural model on the same device. You have chosen to test this rather than assume it: "lets see how small we can usefully go".
Assumption C, withdrawn. Determinism is not a regulatory requirement. It is a chosen principle: "Deterministic and mechanical is fast. The one thing I think we might have over the rest." That is a design value, and it stands.
Assumption D, measured. The archive's language table, read while logged in, gives Afrikaans 8,959 base forms, 13,768 word forms, 17 inflectional paradigms and 7 subcategorisation frames, 42 grammar rules in all. English has 209,178 base forms and a grammar at level B1. Dutch has 4,595 base forms and no grammar. The Afrikaans generation grammar has to be written; the English one can be studied as the model. Your own 2012–2013 uploads are on the Files page and download as plain text.
Assumption E, deferred. Long-term rule maintenance rests on contributors who arrive after the proof of concept. The risk is not gone; it is scheduled.

Licence packages · new this turn
L1 Open Gate S Eliminated
Struck by Question 17: "preventing misuse" needs more than attribution. Under Apache-2.0 a closed fork is legal, and that is the misuse you named.
What it is
Engine under Apache-2.0. Data under CC BY-SA 4.0. Contributors sign the Apache individual CLA once. The name Verstaan is registered as a trademark, or at least carried by a published name policy and the domain.
Pros
  • A radio or phone maker can embed the engine in closed firmware, which is how it reaches a peacekeeping unit.
  • Every contributor grants a patent licence, so nobody can later sue users over a rule-compilation technique.
  • The CLA keeps your right to change the licence later.
Cons
  • A closed fork is legal. "No one can claim it" is enforced by attribution and the trademark, not by copyleft.
  • Apertium's GPL dictionaries can never enter the data store.
  • A CLA is a signature some volunteers refuse on principle.
Best when
Reach onto devices matters more than forcing improvements back.
Afterwards
A NOTICE file, a CLA bot on every first pull request, and a trademark line in the README.
L2 Locked Commons M Runner-up
What it is
Engine under GPLv3. Adapted data relicensed from CC BY-SA 4.0 to GPLv3, which Creative Commons permits one-way, so the whole repository is one licence. Contributors sign off with the DCO. No CLA, and no later relicensing.
Pros
  • Nobody can ship a closed fork; every engine change that ships must be published. This is the legal form of "no one can claim it".
  • One licence for code and data; Apertium's GPL Afrikaans and Dutch dictionaries can be imported.
  • DCO is the lightest possible contributor process.
Cons
  • Closed firmware cannot embed the engine; the basic-comms tier reaches only devices whose makers accept GPL.
  • "Can change later" is gone after the first outside merge.
  • Phone app stores and GPLv3 have a history of friction.
Best when
Keeping the initiative open matters more than reaching closed devices, and the Apertium data is wanted.
Afterwards
A single LICENSE file, a sign-off line on every commit, and one import script that pulls Apertium's Afrikaans dictionary.
L3 Shared Files M ★ Recommended
What it is
Engine under MPL-2.0, a file-level copyleft: anyone who changes an engine source file must publish that file, but may link the engine into a closed application. Data under CC BY-SA 4.0. Contributors sign the Apache individual CLA.
Pros
  • Engine improvements come back; the app around the engine may stay closed, so device makers can still ship it.
  • MPL-2.0 is compatible with both GPL and Apache code, so archived tool source under either could be linked.
  • The CLA keeps your relicensing right.
Cons
  • Least known of the three; contributors and lawyers read it more slowly.
  • A closed fork that adds new files and changes none is legal.
  • Apertium data still cannot enter a CC BY-SA store.
Best when
You want engine changes shared without blocking closed devices.
Afterwards
A licence header on every engine source file, a CLA bot, and a trademark line in the README.

04

Recommendation

Best path

Build the UNL-native engine as the product, in three compile-time tiers, with a neural fallback held back for the connected tier until the rules have been measured. License it as Shared Files: the engine under MPL-2.0, the data store under CC BY-SA 4.0 with each item's original version recorded, contributors signing the Apache individual CLA once, and the name Verstaan registered as a trademark or covered by a published name policy. Phase 1 mirrors every language in the archive, not three, and rewrites the documentation into an agent-readable corpus with the originals kept beside it for attribution.

Why this one

  • It matches the misuse you named. Under MPL-2.0 anyone who changes an engine file and ships it must publish that file. A company cannot take the engine, improve it in private, and sell the improvement as theirs. That is the claim you want to prevent, and file-level copyleft prevents it.
  • It keeps the device reach. A radio or phone maker may link the engine into a closed application, so the peacekeeping, medical and rescue cases stay open. GPLv3 would close them for any maker whose firmware is locked.
  • It keeps your freedom to change. The CLA means you can move to GPLv3 later if closed forks become a real problem, or to Apache-2.0 if reach becomes the problem. Locked Commons gives that up on the first outside merge.
  • The data boundary is already forced. Generated C++ from BY-SA data is an adaptation of the data and carries BY-SA whatever the engine licence says. Shared Files makes that boundary a build rule: the data library is one artefact, the engine is another, and each carries its own LICENSE.
  • The archive is wide enough on its own. With the whole archive mirrored, the loss of Apertium's GPL dictionaries costs less than it did when Afrikaans and Dutch were the only sources. English, French, Spanish, Russian, Arabic and German each carry tens of thousands of base forms and a grammar, and those are the reference set for drafting Afrikaans rules.

Runner-up, and when it wins

Locked Commons wins if either of two things happens. First, a closed fork appears and the trademark and file-level copyleft turn out not to be enough; GPLv3 closes the whole-product loophole that MPL leaves. Second, Apertium's Afrikaans and Dutch dictionaries turn out to be the fastest route to a working demo, because only a GPLv3 store can take them. The CLA makes the move possible; the move is one commit and a notice.

Confidence

●●○ Two of three. The engine shape and the data boundary are firm. The licence choice rests on a reading of "preventing misuse" as "no closed fork of the engine"; if you meant something else, say so and the package changes.

What would change this

  • The archived tool source arrives from the administrator under GPLv2-only. MPL-2.0 can link to it, but the combined work becomes GPL, and the tier story needs re-checking.
  • The CC BY-SA 2.5 Switzerland items turn out to be the bulk of the data. Version 2.5 is not GPLv3-compatible, which removes Locked Commons as a runner-up and makes Shared Files the only workable package.
  • A funded partner requires a specific licence as a condition of funding.

Done when

  • A repository with LICENSE (MPL-2.0) at the root, data/LICENSE (CC BY-SA 4.0), a CLA document and a CLA check on pull requests, and a trademark line in the README.
  • A Phase 1 mirror under data/archive/ holding every language's dictionary, grammar and corpus exports, each with its source URL, licence version and retrieval date in a manifest.
  • An agent-readable docs/unl-reference/ built from the wiki's UNL2010 specs and the grammar documentation, with the original pages kept.
  • A segment specification for the rule compiler that states the interpreted runtime and the compiled build must produce identical output for the same rule set, tested.

The archive: status, contents and licence, verified this turn

The UNL Archive announced itself by e-mail on 7 November 2025. The message states the UNDL Foundation ceased activities in 2024 and that the archive is "a personal and voluntary initiative", run by the Foundation's former Language Resources Manager. E-mail from admin@unlarchive.org, 2025-11-07, in your Gmail export.

The archive's home page describes six sections: language resources, computational resources, documentation, education, the UNL Programme, and the UNDL Foundation. Materials are "provided as is, with no further development or support". UNL Archive home, https://unlarchive.org/.

The terms page, section 6, reads: "You are free to share (copy and redistribute) and adapt (remix, transform, build upon) the Materials for any purpose, including commercial use", and "you must distribute your contributions under the same license (CC BY-SA) or a compatible open license". Section 7 requires attribution to the original author and a statement that the material came from the UNL Archive with a link. Section 8 keeps copyright with the original holders. UNL Archive terms, https://unlarchive.org/index.php?unlweb=terms.

The language-resources page describes UNLarium, the platform that held dictionaries, grammars, corpora and knowledge bases for "more than 100 languages", with dictionary benchmark tiers from ">5,000" to ">100,000 base forms". Data is stated to be under CC BY-SA. Afrikaans and Dutch are not named on the public page; the original UNLarium "required accreditation through VALERIE". UNLarium, https://unlarchive.org/index.php?unlweb=unlarium. Coverage of Afrikaans specifically: no primary source found yet, treat as unknown until the login crawl.

Argos Translate is an offline neural MT library built on OpenNMT, with downloadable models for on-device use. Argos Open Tech, https://www.argosopentech.com/. Licensing the code half, verified this turn

Creative Commons says of its own licences: "We recommend against using Creative Commons licenses for software", because "CC licenses do not contain specific terms about the distribution of source code". Creative Commons FAQ, "Can I apply a Creative Commons license to software?", https://creativecommons.org/faq/. So the engine takes a software licence and the data stays CC BY-SA; the two halves are separate works and the boundary between them is a build artefact, not a folder.

Creative Commons declares two licences compatible with CC BY-SA 4.0: the Free Art License 1.3 and GPLv3. The GPLv3 compatibility is one-way: "you may license your contributions to adaptations of BY-SA 4.0 materials under GPLv3, but you may not license your contributions to adaptations of GPLv3 projects under BY-SA 4.0." Creative Commons, compatible licenses, https://creativecommons.org/share-your-work/licensing-considerations/compatible-licenses/. In practice: archive data can flow into a GPLv3 data store beside Apertium's GPL dictionaries, but Apertium data can never flow into a CC BY-SA store.

On the name: Apache-2.0 section 6 reads "This License does not grant permission to use the trade names, trademarks, service marks, or product names of the Licensor". MPL-2.0 section 2.3 likewise grants no trademark rights. A copyright licence protects the code; only a trademark protects the word Verstaan. Apache-2.0, https://www.apache.org/licenses/LICENSE-2.0; MPL-2.0, https://www.mozilla.org/en-US/MPL/2.0/.

On "can change later": a sole copyright holder can relicense future versions at will; already-released versions stay under their licence forever. Once outside contributions are merged, relicensing needs every contributor's consent unless a contributor licence agreement collected that right at the door. The Developer Certificate of Origin, used by the Linux kernel, is a sign-off that the contributor has the right to submit under the project's current licence; it does not grant relicensing rights. Developer Certificate of Origin, https://developercertificate.org/. The Apache Software Foundation's individual CLA is the reference example of an agreement that does grant a licence to the project broad enough to relicense. Apache ICLA, https://www.apache.org/licenses/icla.pdf. GitHub's plain-language comparison of the four licences is the quickest primary reference for the table above. choosealicense.com, https://choosealicense.com/licenses/.

Controlled language, for the basic-comms tier

ASD-STE100 is the aerospace industry's controlled English: a fixed vocabulary and a constrained grammar, maintained for decades. It is the working proof that a bounded language subset can reach full deterministic coverage, and it is the right model for the first basic-comms build even though option E is struck. ASD-STE100, https://asd-ste100.org/.

Where sources agree and disagree

The archive's own framing and your answers agree: rule-based UNL is a second lineage worth keeping alive, and the data is free to build on. The archive and Apertium disagree only on licence family, CC BY-SA versus GPL, and that is a real incompatibility if dictionaries are merged into one store. No source resolves the Afrikaans coverage question; only the login crawl will.
